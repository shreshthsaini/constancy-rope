Our first campaign used \fluxdev, on which \dype{} and \constancy{} produced a dense halftone lattice over the $4096^2$ frame and \ntk{} a featureless blur. To tell a bug in our port from a property of the model, we ran the official \dype{} code unchanged. It produces the same lattice on \fluxdev{} and a clean image on \krea{} (\cref{fig:checkpoint}), the checkpoint that the official \dype{} and \sega{} scripts load. We moved every experiment to \krea{} and discarded the \fluxdev{} pilot.

Mechanism measurements need not transfer either: on one dev prompt, raising the gain of the fastest octave sharply lowers that band's power on \fluxdev{} and slightly raises it on \krea{}, although guidance and code revision also differ between the two probes (\cref{app:mech}).

\subsection{\sega: paper versus code}\label{sec:papercode}

With the printed law (\cref{eq:sega,tab:papercode}), the allocation is unbounded, and at $4096^2$ the physical bin map sends every pair with a wavelength above \N{c@tokside} tokens, \N{c@collapsedpairs} of the \N{c@pairsperaxis} per axis, to the same frequency bin. The released law scales the spread by 0.15 and clamps the result at 1, and its logit gain stays between $\NmechSegaGainMin$ and $\NmechSegaGainMax$ on our held-out runs. In the eight-prompt dev pilot (descriptive only; \cref{app:pilot}), our implementation of the printed equations scores worse than the released code on every metric; freezing the printed allocation into a static tilt destroys the images, while freezing the released allocation costs much less (\cref{fig:papercode}). Readers who reimplement \sega{} from the paper will not reproduce the released method, and comparisons against \sega{} should state which version they ran.

\subsection{Pitfalls in 4K evaluation}\label{sec:pitfalls}\label{sec:spectra}

Besides the flow shift of \cref{sec:background}, two details changed what our numbers can show or what they cost.

\paragraph{224-pixel scorers miss the finest octaves}
CLIP, PickScore and ImageReward see a 224-pixel view, whose Nyquist frequency is \N{nyq@cpp} cycles per original pixel ($2^{\N{nyq@log}}$); bands \N{nyq@firstband} to 10 of our 11 octave bands lie entirely above it. These are the bands where the arms differ (\cref{fig:spectrum,tab:octave}): in band 7, \ntk{} falls far below the reference profile (log-power error \N{oct@ntk@b7}), the two arms with \sega's allocation stay closest to it (\segaofficial{} \N{oct@sega@b7}, \segactm{} \N{oct@sctm@b7}), and \tempsb{} carries excess power (\N{oct@tsb@b7}). This is why \tempsb{} can tie \segaofficial{} on all three reward models while trailing it on full-frame MUSIQ, and why a 4K comparison that reports only these scorers cannot detect a change in fine detail.

\paragraph{Attention kernel}
On the same GH200 node and method, forcing FlashAttention-2 makes a $4096^2$ sample take \NcompFlashRatio{} times as long as cuDNN-first SDPA (medians \NcompGHflashSec\,s and \NcompGHcudnnSec\,s), while method families on one node differ by at most \NcompFamilySpreadPct\%; one GB200 GPU is \NcompBWSpeedup{} times faster than a GH200 (\cref{tab:compute}). Timings of different methods are comparable only with the kernel and the GPU fixed. Our records did not log the dispatched kernel or a hash of the loaded weights; we recovered both from code revisions and task scripts (\cref{app:compute}).
