\subsection{\sega: paper versus code}\label{sec:papercode}

\paragraph{The printed law is unbounded}
With the printed law (\cref{eq:sega,tab:papercode}) the allocation is unbounded. At $4096^2$ the physical bin map sends every pair with a wavelength above \N{c@tokside} tokens, \N{c@collapsedpairs} of the \N{c@pairsperaxis} per axis, to the same frequency bin. The released law scales the spread by 0.15 and clamps the result at 1, so its logit gain stays between $\NmechSegaGainMin$ and $\NmechSegaGainMax$ on our held-out runs; the printed law, frozen as a static tilt in the dev pilot, spans $\NmechSegaStaticGainMin$ to $\NmechSegaStaticGainMax$.

\paragraph{The printed law scores worse in a dev pilot}
In the eight-prompt dev pilot (descriptive only; \cref{app:pilot}), our implementation of the printed equations scores worse than the released code on every metric, with ImageReward \pim{segap}{ir} against \pim{sega}{ir} (\pid{segap}{ir} \N{pi@ci@segap@ir}). Freezing the printed allocation into a static tilt destroys images (CLIP \pim{segapst}{clip} against \pim{sega}{clip}), while freezing the released allocation costs much less (CLIP \pim{segast}{clip}; \cref{fig:papercode}). Readers who reimplement \sega{} from the paper will not reproduce the released method, so comparisons against \sega{} should state which version they ran.

\subsection{Pitfalls in 4K evaluation}\label{sec:pitfalls}\label{sec:spectra}\label{sec:checkpoint}

\paragraph{224-pixel scorers miss the finest octaves}
CLIP, PickScore and ImageReward see a 224-pixel view with Nyquist frequency \N{nyq@cpp} cycles per original pixel ($2^{\N{nyq@log}}$). Bands \N{nyq@firstband} to 10 of our 11 octaves lie entirely above it, and the methods differ there: band 8's log-power error against the references is \N{oct@tsb@b8} for \tempsb{} and \N{oct@sega@b8} for \segaofficial{} (\cref{fig:spectrum_main,tab:octave}). This may help explain reward-model ties next to a full-frame MUSIQ gap. Resizing can keep indirect effects or alias fine patterns, but these scorers cannot directly resolve original detail and do not suffice alone at 4K.

\paragraph{Checkpoint comparisons need matched guidance}
Our first campaign used \fluxdev, on which \dype{} and \constancy{} produced a dense halftone lattice over the $4096^2$ frame and \ntk{} a featureless blur. Official \dype{} code reproduced the lattice on dev prompt 0, seed 42, at guidance 3.5, while the matching \krea{} run at guidance 4.5 had no visible lattice (\cref{fig:checkpoint}). Our port is therefore not a necessary cause of that failure, but the guidance difference prevents isolating the checkpoint. Like \dype's main experiments~\citep{issachar2025dype}, all our held-out comparisons use \krea; the \fluxdev{} pilot is descriptive evidence only.

\paragraph{The attention kernel costs more than the method}
On one GH200 node, forcing FlashAttention-2 raises the median time per sample from \NcompGHcudnnSec\,s with cuDNN-first SDPA to \NcompGHflashSec\,s, \NcompFlashRatio{} times as long, while method families on one node differ by at most \NcompFamilySpreadPct\% (\cref{tab:cost}); one GB200 GPU takes \NcompBWSec\,s, \NcompBWSpeedup{} times faster than a GH200. Timing comparisons between methods must therefore fix both kernel and GPU. Our records did not log the dispatched kernel or a hash of the loaded weights, so we reconstructed attention policy and transformer identity from code revisions and task scripts, which are not direct runtime records.
