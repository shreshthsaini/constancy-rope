\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Diffusion transformers trained at about one megapixel are routinely asked for $4096\times4096$ images, \N{c@tokratio} times more image tokens than any training sample. Fine-tuning at that size is expensive, so training-free methods edit the rotary position embedding (RoPE)~\citep{su2024roformer} at inference: they rescale its frequencies~\citep{chen2023pi,bloc97ntk,peng2024yarn}, schedule the rescaling with the noise level~\citep{issachar2025dype}, sharpen attention with a global logit temperature~\citep{jin2023training,nakanishi2025ssmax} and, most recently, scale each rotary band by a gain read from the spectrum of the current latent~\citep{sega2026}. Each method is evaluated as a package against the previous one, and the improvement is credited to its newest component.

We ask where the improvement comes from. We take \sega~\citep{sega2026}, a strong recent method with released code, on the checkpoint its authors use (\krea), and separate its three components: a strengthened NTK frequency base, a global logit temperature and a content-adaptive allocation of per-band q/k gains. All arms share prompts, seeds, schedule and sampler; a dev/held-out split was fixed before any held-out score was inspected, and every comparison is paired over \N{ho@nprompts} held-out prompts with bootstrap intervals and a seed-1 replication. We also test whether more principled per-band laws do better: from efficient coding and contrast constancy we derive four controllers, select among them on the dev set under a written gate, and evaluate the survivors held out.

The answers are mostly negative for the spectral story, and we think they are useful:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Base and temperature carry most of the gain.} Relative to the \ntk{} base alone, \sega's base and global temperature account for \shp{ir} of its ImageReward gain, \shp{pick} of PickScore and \shp{musiqc} of crop MUSIQ; the spectral allocation adds a small but real full-resolution sharpness gain (MUSIQ \hodci{alloc}{musiq}) and nothing measurable on the reward models (\cref{sec:decomp}).
\item \textbf{Principled per-band laws do not help.} None of the four vision-derived controllers beats \segaofficial; the best, \segactm, ties it on ImageReward and full-resolution MUSIQ and loses on PickScore (\hod{sctm}{pick}) and crop MUSIQ (\hod{sctm}{musiqc}). We trace the failure to a visibility gate that stays shut for most of sampling, a native-resolution target that trades sharpness for spectral match, a mostly fixed \sega{} allocation and a weak, prompt-dependent gain actuator (\cref{sec:laws}).
\item \textbf{Results depend on the checkpoint.} \fluxdev{} renders a halftone lattice at $4096^2$ even under the official \dype{} code, while \krea{} is clean, and a mechanism probe reverses sign between them (\cref{sec:checkpoint}).
\item \textbf{\sega's paper and code differ.} The released code changes the feature the sensor reads, the gain law, its clipping and the frequency base; in an eight-prompt dev pilot the printed equations score far below the code, and their static tilt destroys images (\cref{sec:papercode}).
\item \textbf{4K evaluation and engineering have traps.} The default flow shift extrapolates to $\mu=\N{c@mudefault}$ at $4096^2$, leaving the last step at $\sigma=\N{c@sigmalastdefault}$; 224-pixel reward models miss the top \N{nyq@nbands} octaves at 4K; and forced FlashAttention-2 takes \NcompFlashRatio{} times as long as cuDNN-first attention (\cref{sec:pitfalls}).
\end{enumerate}
We do not propose a new state-of-the-art method. Our contribution is the controlled decomposition, a negative result with its mechanisms, and a protocol that makes such claims checkable. Code, per-image scores and the scripts that generate every table and number are released with the paper.
