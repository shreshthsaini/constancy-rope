\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Diffusion transformers trained at about one megapixel are routinely asked for $4096\times4096$ images, \N{c@tokratio} times more tokens than any training image. Training-free methods make this work by editing the rotary position embedding (RoPE)~\citep{su2024roformer} at inference: they rescale its frequencies~\citep{chen2023pi,bloc97ntk,peng2024yarn}, schedule the rescaling with the noise level~\citep{issachar2025dype}, sharpen attention with a global logit temperature~\citep{jin2023training,nakanishi2025ssmax} and, most recently, scale each rotary band by a gain read from the spectrum of the current latent~\citep{sega2026}. Each method is compared as a package with the previous one, and the improvement is credited to its newest component.

We ask where the improvement comes from. We take \sega~\citep{sega2026}, a strong recent method with released code, on the checkpoint its authors use (\krea), and compare its base and global temperature together against its content-adaptive per-band allocation of query and key gains. All arms share prompts, seeds, schedule and sampler; the dev/held-out split was fixed before any held-out score was seen; every main comparison is paired over \N{ho@nprompts} held-out prompts with bootstrap intervals, and a seed-1 replication covers \N{so@n@tsb} of them. We then test whether per-band laws with a principled basis beat \sega's heuristic: from efficient coding and contrast constancy we derive four controllers, use the dev set to choose variants, and evaluate the survivors held out. A written advancement gate applied from the second campaign onward. Our findings, in order of strength:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Base and temperature carry most of the gain} (\cref{fig:hero}). Of \sega's gain over the \ntk{} base, its base and global temperature alone carry \shp{ir} on ImageReward, \shp{pick} on PickScore and \shp{musiqc} on crop MUSIQ. The per-band allocation adds a full-frame MUSIQ gain (\hodci{alloc}{musiq}) alongside less excess fine-scale power, with no detected reward-model gain (\cref{sec:decomp}). MUSIQ is a learned quality proxy, not a sharpness measurement.
\item \textbf{Principled per-band laws do not beat the heuristic.} The two controllers that reached the full held-out set tie or lose to \segaofficial{} on every reward and image-quality metric; the closer one, \segactm, ties it on ImageReward and full-frame MUSIQ and loses on PickScore (\hod{sctm}{pick}) and crop MUSIQ (\hod{sctm}{musiqc}). Trajectory diagnostics suggest possible causes: a gate that opens late, a target that improves spectral match while lowering MUSIQ, an allocation that is mostly a fixed tilt and a prompt-dependent response to per-band gain changes (\cref{sec:laws}).
\item \textbf{Checkpoint comparisons need matched settings.} In the development comparison, \fluxdev{} renders a halftone lattice at $4096^2$ even under official \dype{} code, while \krea{} does not. Guidance also differs, so the contrast does not isolate a checkpoint effect (\cref{sec:checkpoint}).
\item \textbf{\sega's paper and code differ.} The released code changes the sensor input, the gain law, its bounds and the frequency base. In a descriptive eight-prompt pilot the printed equations score worse than the released code on every metric, and their static tilt destroys images (\cref{sec:papercode}).
\item \textbf{4K evaluation has traps.} 224-pixel reward models cannot see the top \N{nyq@nbands} octaves of a 4K image; the default flow shift extrapolates to $\mu=\N{c@mudefault}$, so every model call sees almost pure noise; and attention backend changes sampling cost (\cref{sec:pitfalls}).
\end{enumerate}
We contribute the controlled decomposition, negative results with diagnostic evidence, and a protocol that makes these claims checkable. Code, per-image scores and the scripts that generate every table and number in the paper accompany the paper.
